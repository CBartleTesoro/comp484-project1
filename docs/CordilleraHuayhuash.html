The Hidden Treasure of the Andes

Geographers have traditionally divided the highest of the Peruvian Andes into twenty cordilleras, or glaciated mountain chains. The most prominent among these include the 180-km long Cordillera Blanca in the department of Ancash, crowned by Peru's highest peak, 6768-meter Huascaran; the Cordulleras Vilcanota and Carabaya to the east of Cusco, dominated by the monolithic Nevado (Mt.) Ausangate; and the legendary Cordullera Vilcabamba, where the Inca Trail winds its way through the cloud forest beneath the icy walls of Nevados Salcantay and Wakay Willka toward the sanctuary of Machu Picchu.

A Magnificent Andes

One range just south of the Cordillera Blanca may look insignificant on most maps of Peru. It runs only thirty kilometers of Lima, Ancash and Huanuco. On a clear day, the highest summits of this range can be spotted in the distance from Conococha, the high point of the route from the coast to Huaraz, but otherwise cannot be seen from any major town or road.

Yet this compact range—the Cordillera Huayhuash—is perhaps the most magnificent in all of the Peruvian Andes.

From a distance the Huayhuash appears to be a single massive mountain—the 6634m (21,766 ft.) Yerupaja, second highest in Peru and the highest point of the vast Amazon watershed—surrounded by a number of lesser summits. Yet as one approaches the range it becomes apparent that there are more than a dozen serrated summits along a single ice-clad ridge, each with its own distinct beauty. Upon reaching the heart of the range one discovers lakes, streams, forests, wildlife and flowers of the greatest delicacy. It is a wondrous region, from the grandest of scales to the most minute.

Unique landscape and wildlife

Six peaks surpass 6000 meters (19,686 ft.) and another fifteen measure more than 5400 meters (17,717 ft.) The cirques and valleys at the base of the mountains are dotted with sparkling glacial lakes at altutudes between 4000 and 4500 meters. Many of these lakes are filled with trout and support abundant birdlife. Much of the terrain is rugged grassland or puna, but there are also native queñual (polylepsis) forests harboring several threatened or near-threatened species of birds.

Ancentral mountain communities

The Huayhuash (a Quechua word for a type of weasel, pronunced simply “Why wash”) is also home to a number of ancestral mountain comunities. Scattered through the valleys on the western slopes are four villages—LLamac, Pocpa,Pacllon and Huayllapa—and, at higher elevation, occasional herder's huts known as chozas. The local people tend herds of livestock, produce cheese famed throught the region, catch fish in the lakes and streams, and cultivate surprisingly diverse and abundant Andean crops. The stark eastern slope of the range is more sparsely populated, mostly by seasonal herders from the town of Queropalca and the comunity of Jesus watching over their herds of attle, sheep and alpacas. Many residents also supplement their income by working as guides, cooks or arrieros (burro drivers) for the trekkers and climbers who come from around the world. The people of the Huayhuash are open and welcoming to visitors, often inviting them to share in local customs such as the typical feast of the pachamanca (“pot of the earth”), where spiced meat and potatoes are baked underground.

A spectacular mountain trek

The 165-km circuit around the Huayhuash massif is one of the most spectacular mountain treks anywhere. Though a strenuous trip, normally requiring twelve days and crossing five major passes, it transports hikers into a natural world of unsurpassed beuty, enhanced by the presence of the traditional Andean communities. Hikers and climbers have been returning in ever greater numbers since 1993, when the threat of terrorism which had paralyzed the region for the previous six years subsided. People without the time or inclination to talk the entire loop can still visit one of the most breathtaking spots in the Huayhuash by hiking the two-day route to Laguna Jahuachocha from the trailhead at the town of Chiquian.
