The Book:

The book is a small hand guide, with 4.25" x 6.87" dimentions, and is 159 pages long. The book contains:

  • Descriptions of 26 hikes, more tan 1000 kilometers of trails, among the 6000-meter peaks of the cordilleras of north-central Peru.
  • 16 pages of photographs.
  • 11 individual trail maps.
  • Foldout map of the entire region.
  • General information for foreign visitors on transportation, climate, mountain conditions, health, availability of supplies, the local people, etc.

Introduction

After riding the bus for four hours on the rought, winding climb from Casma on the Peruvian Coast, the traveler reaches the crest of the Cordillera Negra and looks across the Santa Valley, or Callejon de Huayias, to the Cordillera Blanca. However well prepared, he cannot have anticipated the impact of seeing the towering white mountain range spread across the horizon. It is hardly less dazzling to arrive by the more comon route on the paved highway through Pativilca and Conocoha, or to drive through the hart of the Callejon from Huaraz to Caraz, or to see the Cordillera Huayhuash, dominated by the massive Yerupaja, from the winding road descending to Chiquian.

But these dramatic vistas can only give the palest hint f what it is like to walk through the deep canyons leadning into the Cordilleras and enter and alpine world of ice-covered 6000-meter peaks, long meadows, glacial lakes, rivers and waterfalls. This is not as difficult as it may seem; despite their forbidding appearance, these mountain ranges are surprisingly hospitable, offering good weather most days between May and mid-September, often later. And the mountains are quite accessibe; the main towns, Huaraz and Chiquian, are an easy eight-hour bus ride north from the capital of Lima, and the backpacker can ussually be walking among the high peaks half a day after leaving the trailhead.

Add to this the wonderfull people living in the remote villages, the fascinating plant and animal life, and the remains of Inca and pre-Inca cultures, and the result is a world so enchanting that most foreign visitors vow to return soon.

The trails in these ranges are build by and for the local people or by Ingemment, a goverment agency responsible for constrolling the glacial lakes. Not one kilometer of one trail has yet been built or improved for touristics purposes. The routes are confusing, sometimes without trails, and always (to this date (1980)) without any signs or trail markers. Most parts are rarely visited by backpackers or climbers, and many who do go travel in large, unwieldy groups, complete with guides, cooks and strings of burros, causing much of the wilderness experience to be lost.

This sort of expedition is not necessary. Well-prepared small groups or individuals can visit virtually any part of either the Cordillera Blanca or the Cordillera Huayhuash without difficulty. The only obstacle has been in obtaining enough reliable information to choose a trip and then do it without needing to hire a guide.

The purpose of this book is to provide this information. It describes 1000 kilometers of of trails, ranging in difficulty from easy walks in the Quebradas (Canyons) Paron and Yanapaccha among the greatest peaks of the Cordillera Blanca to a strenuous 180-km hike around the entire Cordillera Huayhuash. Altough you will have a general idea of what to expect before starting a trip, the detailed descriptions of the routes make no mention of the scenery — you will be discovering the sights for yourself.

This is not a cultural or scientific study — exellent books dealing with these aspecs are already available, notably “Cordillera Blanca” and “Cordillera Huayhuash” by Hans Kinzl and Erwin Schneider and “Yuraq Janka” by John Ricker. It is simply a practical guide to the area, filled with the information the foreign visitor will need.

No guide book can serve as more than an aide to the hiker. It cannot be a substitute for mountain competence or experience, a general sense of direction, or basic common sense. The hiker also must choose a trip at a level appropriate to his ability, experience and physical condition.

Altough these mountains are generally hospitable and gentle, it must be remembered that they are still among the world's great alpine regions and must be approached with the caution and respect they are due.

Using this Guide

Most of the trail descriptions are divided into three parts:

  1. An Introduction — Information to help the hiker choose a trip:
    attractions, level of difficulty, trail conditions, etc.
  2. A Table of Distances and Altitutes — Distances between points along the trail are educated guesses; altitudes are estimated to the nearest 50m. Names of trails off the main route are indented and the distances and altitudes placed in parentheses
  3. A detailed route Description — How to follow the trail, places to camp, sources of water, etc. Sights are generally not described exept on side trips. Views are mentioned in a few cases where they serve as aides in following the route.

CAPITALS refer to places listed in the distance and altitude table above.

(xx,xx,xxxx) — km from previous point, total km, altitude, as listed in the table. (The first point along the trail has only one distance listed)

(xxxx) — Altitude in meters: altitudes given are those which are most generally accepted, ussually taken from Ingemmet maps. Altitudes ending in 00 or 50 are estimates (with the exeption of Nevado Pilanco, which apparently is exactly 5300m).

Italics indicate side trips off main route.

  • $ — US dollars; all prices are estimated in dollars(in which prices are more stable than in Peruvian soles).
    Note: Any $ estimate in the book is no longer accuarte, as it doesnt account for 46 years of inflation and economic changes.
  • km — kilometers (1km = 0.62 miles).
  • m — meters (1m = 3.28 feet.)
  • Route — a described course for hiking; may be a road, established trail, path, or completly trailless.
  • Main trail — principal route as described in this guide, altough other routes may be clear or more heavily traveled.
  • Alternate route — another route which leads to the same point.
  • Side trip — a route leading away from the main trail to a point of interest.
  • Cairn — trail marker, ussually a pile of rocks.
  • Switchback — zig-zag trail going up a steep slope.

The dificulty rating of the trails takes into account both the diffuclty of the terrain and the length of the hike. Altough my Standards may differ from yours, all the trails are judged comparatively one to another — if you walk one trail rated "moderate", you will know that another trail rated “moderate to easy” is somewhat easier. But no matter what your standard of dificulty is, you will find any trail rated “difficult” to be streneous. Be sure to you are adecuately prepared before making any of the following hikes: Pyramid of Alpamayo, Alto de Pucaraji, going over the Pasaje de Ulta, Laguna Legiacocha, and especially the circuit of the Cordillera Huayhuash.

Unhurried, leisruley walking covers about 3 km per hour. Steady, purposeful walking with occasional short rests is about 4 km per hour. It is diffucult to cover more than 5 km per hour even when walking as fast as possible and not sottping at all.
Burros ussually go a bit faster than 3km per hour, but unlike humans, do not slow noticeably when going up the steep parts.

Some rivers, lakes, passes and mountains have more than one name (or spelling of its name). The names used by local people will often be different from those in the book (some mountains have different names on every side). Probably half of the names on the Instituto Geografico Militar maps — names unheard of elsewhere — are different from those used here. I have used the names and spellings most commnly placed on other maps and used by people with extensive knowledge of the region.
